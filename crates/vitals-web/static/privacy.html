What this site knows about you.

Every sentence below was written by reading the code that does the thing it describes, not from a template. Where this page and the code disagree, one of them is a bug and we want to hear which. There is no sign-up here, no password, and no real patient — so most of what a privacy policy usually has to cover does not exist. What is left is unusual enough to be worth reading properly, and the first section is the one that cannot be undone.

Read this before you press it

Anchoring a run is permanent and public, and nobody can undo it — not you, not us.

One button on this site writes to a public blockchain: anchor this run on chain, at the end of a finished case. It is off by default, it is never automatic, and you can play every case here for the rest of your life without ever pressing it. Nothing else on this site puts anything on a chain.

When you do press it, the record is copied to every machine that keeps that chain. We cannot delete it, edit it, hide it or correct it, and neither can you. There is no takedown, no support ticket and no key we hold that would help. If you anchor a run you later regret, the only thing anyone can do is anchor better runs beside it. The bad one stays.

What a stranger reading the chain can see, per anchored run

  • your player public key — the key your browser made (§2)
  • the case, as a SHA-256 hash. The case files are public in our repository, so anyone can hash them and match: in practice this names which case you played
  • the difficulty tier — student, intern or resident
  • whether you declared it an exam or practice, decided before you played
  • the outcome, including whether the patient died and which way
  • the number of harm events the engine recorded against you
  • the deterministic score and what it was out of
  • the hash of your pre-run commitment and the slot it was made at — the proof that you declared the attempt before you knew how it would go
  • a hash of the rubric used to mark it, and a hash of the tape

If you then prove that run and claim a level, three more things exist on chain under your key, permanently: a claim record repeating the case, score, maximum, deterministic score, tier and exam flag for each proven attempt; a progress record holding your level, your distinct-case count, your attempt count and your XP; and a commitment account holding a count of every attempt you have ever declared. That count only ever goes up. Clearing a commitment does not reduce it. It is deliberate — it is what stops somebody committing five runs and anchoring only the good one — and it means the chain permanently knows how many times you started, whether or not you finished.

What a stranger reading the chain cannot see

  • your name, your email, your school or your face — we never ask for any of them
  • the words you typed to the patient, and the words the patient said back. The tape is committed to as a hash; the text itself is never published
  • your IP address
  • which real person the key belongs to

That last one is the whole risk, and it is yours to manage. The key is a pseudonym only for as long as you keep it one. The moment you tell anyone that key is yours — put it on a CV, hand it to a residency programme, post it — everything in the first list above is permanently and publicly attached to your name, including the runs where the patient died. That is exactly the feature: a record a stranger can check without trusting us. It is also exactly the cost. Decide which runs you want attached to you before you anchor them, because afterwards there is no mechanism.

Which chain, honestly. This bay anchors to Solana devnet today — a public test network. Devnet carries no money, and its operators reset it from time to time; that is their decision, not ours, and it is not a deletion mechanism you should rely on for anything. Treat what you anchor as permanent, because the intention is to move to mainnet, where it plainly is. The page you anchor from prints which cluster it is writing to, live, so you never have to take this paragraph's word for it.

Identity

There are no accounts, no passwords, and no seed phrase.

Nothing here asks you to sign up. There is no email field, no password to reset and no profile. When your browser first needs an identity it generates an Ed25519 keypair with the browser's own WebCrypto and keeps the private half in that browser's localStorage. The private key never leaves your browser: the server prepares a transaction, your browser signs it, the server submits it. The server cannot produce that signature and holds no key of yours.

You never see a seed phrase because you never need to fund anything. A relay key we own pays every transaction fee — it pays, and it never plays. That is why a nineteen-year-old can anchor a run without buying SOL or installing a wallet.

The consequence, stated plainly: clear your browser's site data and that key is gone. With it goes your ability to prove an anchored run was yours. We cannot recover it, because we never had it. If you play on more than one machine, link the second device to the first from inside the app rather than starting again.

What is kept in your browser and never sent anywhere

Under keys beginning vitals.: your key, which account it belongs to, your chosen language, which episodes you have cleared or starred, your stars and tiers, which cards you have seen, and which teasers have played. Plus vitals-analytics-consent (§8) and vitals-review-draft (§7). All of it is local to that browser. Clearing site data removes all of it.

Patients

There is no patient data here, because there are no patients.

Every patient in Vitals is synthetic — an authored case, not a record of a real person. There is no PHI in this system to protect, leak or lose. That is a design constraint rather than a claim about our security, and it is what makes the rest of this page short.

It cuts the other way too, and §5 says so: because there is no real patient in the loop, the text you type is sent to a cloud model. Do not put anything about a real patient into it.

Runs

What a run stores on our server, and for how long.

Opening a case creates one record on our side, keyed by a random session id. It holds:

FieldWhat it is
epwhich case you are playing
owneryour player public key, when your browser made one. A kiosk or a browser that cannot sign has none, and then the random session id is the only thing protecting the run
sce_hashthe hash of the exact scenario file, so a rewritten case cannot silently resume your run under different rules
tapeeverything you did: every clock tick, every order in the words you typed, every question you asked, every dial you turned, every device you removed, every shock and its joules
saidthe conversation — your question and the patient's reply, kept so the patient remembers what they have already told you
anchoredwhether this run has been written to the chain
committhe commitment hash, the slot it landed at, and the nonce. The nonce stays here and never reaches the chain
exam_modewhether you declared this an exam before playing

The patient's replies are stored but never hashed and never anchored — a model's words are not evidence of anything, so they are kept out of the proof path entirely. Your questions are on the tape, and the tape's hash is what an anchor commits to.

Retention, including the part that does not work yet

The code sweeps away runs that nobody has touched for 24 hours — a closed tab is not a patient. Two limits on that, and we would rather write them down than let the number stand alone:

  • The sweep runs once, when the server starts. It is not a background timer.
  • The sweep only works when runs are kept as files on a disk. The public bay keeps them in Google Firestore, where expiring by age needs a stored timestamp and a query that are not written — so the sweep does nothing there and honestly reports zero.

So on the live site, a run you started is not deleted by age today. That is a gap, we know about it, and stating it is cheaper than implying a deletion that is not happening. If you want a run of yours deleted, mail us and it will be — this is on our server, and we can.

Everything that is not a run is marked durable and is never expired by age, on purpose: the month's meter counts, the usage tally, reviewer submissions, and above all the leaf list. That last one is what every Merkle proof this server issues is rebuilt from — expire it and the anchors on chain survive, provably meaningless. Anything added later defaults to durable too, because an oversight that keeps data uses disk, and an oversight that deletes data destroys it.

The patient's voice

What you type to the patient leaves this machine.

When the patient speaks, a language model is speaking. We prefer to run that model on our own hardware — a local gateway, where nothing leaves the building — and the app uses it whenever it can reach it. The public bay has no GPU, so it uses a cloud model: Google Cloud's Vertex AI, google/gemini-3.1-flash-lite by default, inside the Google Cloud project this service runs in. The status line and the debrief name which model answered.

This is a deliberate exception to our standing rule that inference stays local, recorded as such in the repository. It is defensible for one reason only: the patient is synthetic, so there is no patient data in what is sent.

Exactly what is sent, per question

  • the character brief, built from the public case file — who the patient is, what is true about them, how they should sound, and how ill they are right now
  • the last eight messages of your conversation with them — four exchanges, not the whole run
  • the question you just typed, as typed

Nothing else. Not your key, not your session id, not your name — there isn't one — and not your IP address, because the request is made by our server, not by your browser.

What you type is up to you, and that is the point of saying this. Do not type anything about a real patient, and do not type anything about yourself you would not send to Google. It goes to the model, it goes on the tape, and the tape's hash is what you would be anchoring.

Nothing the model says is ever an input to a score. Same tape, same outcome, same marks, with no language model anywhere in the scoring path.

Rate limiting

What we keep of your network address.

The bay is free and the inference is paid for by donations, so one visitor must not be able to spend the month. Requests are counted per network address — the only thing a stranger cannot rotate for free.

What is retained, precisely

The address itself: the first entry in the X-Forwarded-For header, or the socket's IP when there is no such header. It is used as a key in a map held in the running process's memory, pointing at a list of request timestamps.

Where it is not: it is never written to our store, never to a disk, never to Firestore. Restart the server and every one of them is gone. The application writes no access log of its own and never logs a request.

How long: timestamps older than 24 hours are dropped whenever that address is seen again, and the whole map is pruned once it passes 4,096 addresses. So: 24 hours, in memory, with an entry for an address that never returns sitting idle until the next prune.

What is not kept beside it: no user agent, no cookie, no request path, no identifier, and no link between an address and a run, a key, a score or a review.

Three counters use it, and they are separate: opening runs, submitting reviews, and talking to the patient. Only the last one spends money, and only that one is also capped for the whole bay per month.

One thing that is not ours. This service runs on Google Cloud Run, and the platform keeps its own request logs, which include the source IP address, under Google Cloud's retention for that project. We do not read them for anything but debugging a fault, but they exist and we would rather name them than let "we do not log your IP" stand as the whole story.

The review form

/review is the one place on this site that asks for your name.

That page is sent by hand to a small number of reviewers — a final-year student and a practising physician — and is linked from nowhere. If you are reading this having filled it in, this section is the one that is about you.

What is stored, exactly

  • your role: student or physician
  • your name, if you typed one. It is optional and an unsigned review is still worth reading
  • your contact, if you typed one — whatever you put in the box, as you wrote it
  • the "do not name me" checkbox, as a flag
  • every answer: the question exactly as it was shown to you, which option you chose and the wording of that option, and whatever you wrote
  • the free notes box
  • which build you were reviewing, and the second the server received it by its own clock

What the "do not name me" checkbox does and does not do

It does tell us not to name you in the rubric files we publish. We write "reviewed by a practising physician" instead.

It does not anonymise the record. If you typed a name and a contact, they are stored exactly as you typed them, in the same document as everything else, whether the box is ticked or not. It is a request about how we credit you — not a delete, and not a mask.

If you want us to hold nothing that identifies you, leave the name and contact boxes empty. The form works perfectly that way, and the answers are just as useful.

The form has no login and no caller identity of any kind — deliberately, because requiring an account to tell us what is wrong means the review never arrives. One honest consequence: two people who both leave the name blank and answer identically are stored as a single record, because nothing on that endpoint can tell them apart.

Your draft is saved as you type under vitals-review-draft in your own browser, and it never leaves your device until you press send. A successful send clears it.

What we do with a review: read it, and change the product. That is all. It is never an input to anybody's run, score, mark sheet or anchored record — the code that files a review touches no session, no tape and no tree, and an opinion about the product is data recorded beside a run and never inside one. Reviews are kept rather than expired, because they are documents we act on over months.

You can have yours deleted, or have your name taken out of it. Mail paripol@megawiz.co and it will be done. We can do this because a review lives on our server and not on a chain.

Analytics

Google Analytics, and what happens before you answer the banner.

The landing page, the donate page, the play page and the deck load Google Analytics 4 (G-LH5JSMQBSL) — and only when the hostname ends in vitals.academy. On a local build, a preview URL or anything else, no tag is loaded at all.

Before you answer

The tag is loaded with Google's consent mode set to denied for both analytics and advertising storage. In that state it sets and reads no cookies and stores no identifier on your device — which is what the banner says. Here is the part a banner never says: the tag file is still fetched from googletagmanager.com and events are still sent to Google without storage, and any request to Google reveals your IP address and user agent to Google simply by being a request. "No cookies" is true. "Nothing is sent" would not be.

After you answer

Accept grants analytics storage and GA behaves as ordinary GA — cookies, a client id, the usual. Decline leaves it denied. Either way the choice is remembered in your own browser under vitals-analytics-consent; clear site data and you will be asked again.

What an event contains

The name of the control you clicked and the path of the page. The control's name is deliberately never its visible text — the code reads data-ga, id, aria-label, href or the bare tag name, and never textContent, so a control that could ever render something a learner typed cannot report it.

/review loads no analytics at all, and neither does this page or the terms page.

Fonts

Every page here, this one included, loads its typefaces from fonts.googleapis.com and fonts.gstatic.com. That is a request from your browser to Google, it happens before any banner, and consent does not gate it. A content blocker stops it and every page still reads fine.

The public counters

What we publish, and why nobody is identifiable in it.

Three endpoints are open to anyone: /api/usage, /api/meter and /api/fuel. Open them; the list below is everything in them.

/api/usage
  • runs started, finished, survived, died
  • runs started with no device key
  • starts by case, finishes by outcome
  • a 90-day table of starts and finishes per day
  • distinct browsers seen this month, as a number
  • runs anchored on chain
  • seven sentences saying what those numbers cannot mean
/api/meter and /api/fuel
  • the month, patient turns used, the ceiling, what is left
  • clicks on the donation link
  • the relay's balance and the treasury's SOL and USDC
  • the fee arithmetic, so you can redo the division
  • the anchored count and the tree id

No individual is identifiable in any of it. There is no signup here, so there is nothing that is a person to count — every one of those numbers is runs or devices, and the endpoint says so in its own first field rather than leaving it to this page.

The device count is the size of a set, and the set's contents are never served. Each entry is the first four bytes of sha256(month ‖ your public key) — 32 bits, salted with the month. It cannot be reversed to a key, it de-duplicates one browser within a month and nothing more, and the same browser is a different fingerprint next month. Only the current month and the one before it are kept.

One endpoint is not aggregate, and it is fair to name it: /api/chain?player= followed by a public key reports how many runs that key has proven on this server's current tree. It needs the key to answer, and it reads that number off the chain — where anyone holding the key could already read it.

What this page does not attempt

No lawyer has read this. It is a description of what the software does, written by the people who wrote the software. It does not state your rights under Thailand's PDPA, the GDPR or any other statute, it does not name a governing law or a supervisory authority, and it is not a waiver of anything you are owed under them. Those are questions for a professional, and when one has answered them this page will say so and say who. Until then we would rather leave a gap that is visible than fill it with a clause we invented.
