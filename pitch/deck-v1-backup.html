02the problemVitals
The problem

A doctor's competence stops at the border.

Since 2024, ECFMG certification — the gate to US residency — requires graduating from a school accredited by a WFME-recognised agency. Accreditation is granted to institutions, country by country, and most countries do not have it.

4,350+medical schools in the World Directory
~23countries with a WFME-recognised accrediting agency
130–147countries whose graduates apply for ECFMG certification

For most of the world's medical graduates there is no mechanism to prove competence abroad. Their school's paperwork is the only artifact, and nobody downstream recognises it. Meanwhile every one of them ran hundreds of scored clinical encounters that would have proved it — and all of that was thrown away.

04the insightVitals
The insight

Our progression system is already a pure function.

No database lookup. No server opinion. No randomness. Just arithmetic over the attempt history — already unit-tested, already in Rust.

// engine/src/competency.rs — verbatim, shipping today
pub fn level_for(xp: i64) -> i64 {
    let mut lvl = 1;
    while 25 * (lvl + 1) * (lvl + 2) <= xp { lvl += 1; }
    lvl
}

pub fn dreyfus(avg: f64, distinct: usize, hard: usize, variance: f64) -> &'static str { … }

A function this small runs inside an Anchor program. Which means the chain does not have to take our word for a student's level — it can recompute it.

06commit → encounter → revealVitals
The mechanism

Commit before, replay after.

t0 · commitBefore the encounter starts: hash(case ‖ student ‖ nonce) lands onchain. You cannot practise five times and anchor only the good run — the chain already knows how many you started.
t1 · encounterThe student interviews the virtual patient. Inference runs on our existing backend; nothing clinical is sent anywhere new.
t2 · scoreThe verifier — not the player's device — replays the action trace against the simulation and holds the signing key. A player cannot sign their own outcome.
t3 · revealA compressed leaf over the action trace and what it did to the patient. Pinning the simulation hash is what makes the run re-derivable — by anyone, forever, without us.

The demo: two machines, no shared database, same tape, same patient, same outcome. A certificate cannot do that.

07the world modelVitals
What we actually prove

Don't anchor the grade. Anchor the world.

The patient runs on a deterministic physiology automaton — continuous vital dynamics, triggers, state transitions, harm events, a terminal outcome. It already ships, loaded from JSON, with golden tests that drive it from a replay tape.

outside the proof

The conversation

An LLM plays the patient. It is what makes the encounter worth playing — and it is non-deterministic, so it stays out of the evidence entirely. The transcript never has to be revealed.

inside the proof

The world model

What you did, when you did it, whether the patient was harmed, whether they survived. A verifier replays the same tape against the same simulation and must reach the same outcome.

leaf = hash(sce_id ‖ sce_hash ‖ action_trace ‖ outcome ‖ harm_events)

No model anywhere in the verification path. This is verifiable replay — what chess engines and speedrun leaderboards have done for decades — applied to a patient who is dying on a clock.

08progression, recomputedVitals
The differentiator

Every other achievement token is minted because a server said so.

Ours is minted because the chain recomputed the predicate and agreed. claim_progress takes merkle proofs for anchored attempts, runs its own integer copy of level_for and dreyfus, and mints only if the arithmetic holds.

claim_progress(proofs[]) →
    program recomputes xp, level, distinct_cases, variance
    claim: Expert   ·  computed: Proficient   → REJECTED
    claim: Proficient · computed: Proficient → skill tree advances

Show the rejection first. A protocol that refuses its own user's claim is the fastest way to prove there is no trusted issuer in the loop.

09soulbound by constructionVitals
A deliberate refusal

None of it is tradeable. That's the point.

The design

Token-2022 NonTransferable on every progression token. One evolving skill tree per specialty — advancing Novice → Competent → Proficient → Expert — instead of hundreds of badges cluttering a wallet.

The reason

A tradeable "Expert in Cardiology" is credential fraud with extra steps. A secondary market for it would make the entire system worthless on day one.

We give up the secondary-market volume on purpose. Every pitch reaches for tradability because it makes the tokenomics slide easier; refusing it is what shows we understand the domain.

10escrow · the moneyVitals
Where real money enters

Scholarships that pay out on proof, not paperwork.

A sponsor — a global health NGO, a hospital network recruiting abroad, a specialty college, a diaspora fund — posts a bounty:

"First 100 graduates anywhere to reach EPA 10 · Proficient this year: $150 each."

The escrow releases on a provably attained level. No committee, no application form, no country list, no trusted distributor — and the sponsor verifies every payout without seeing a single student's transcript. A student in a country with no recognised accrediting agency is eligible on exactly the same terms as one in Boston.

Gating

required_badge lives in the registry, not our client — so a competing front-end inherits it for free.

Author royalties

1–5¢ per attempt to whoever wrote the case, paid across borders. Card rails eat that whole amount in fees, and most of the world's clinical educators cannot get an acquiring relationship at all.

Institutions

Schools stay on ordinary invoices in local currency. No university is going to hold USDC, and a design that needs them to is dead on arrival.

The moment

Three distinct cases clears distinct >= 3, so the chain computes Competent. Proficient needs distinct >= 5. Claim Proficient and the program rejects its own user — not because we scripted a failure, but because the real threshold in shipping code says no. Then claim Competent, and the skill tree advances on-screen.

Everything else waits: no leaderboard, no email, no cloud model, no institutional credential, no wallet the student has to understand.

One language, three targets

Rust end to end. The same scoring code compiles into the web app, into the verifier that re-derives a score, and into wasm on the public verify page — with an integer twin inside the Anchor program. The claim "anyone can reproduce this number" is cheap to make and expensive to honour; sharing one implementation is how we honour it.
